“GRRROAAAAAR!”

The magnetic force-shields locked the sector down before the massive beast’s rampage could spread any further.

“GRRROAAAAAR!”

Towers lay in ruins as its mighty, scaly tail swept through them—dust and glass collapsing all around. The streets below vanished beneath rolling smoke. Sirens wailed, then choked into static.

“Grooooaaaaaar…”

After that slightly less intimidating sound, even the mighty creature went still. It turned, slow and offended, toward whoever had dared to answer its violence.

From the dust clouds, a figure rose.

Unfazed by the wind, a dazzling green silhouette stood atop the only building still stubborn enough to remain standing. In one hand: a bark shield, rough and living. In the other: an emerald blade shaped like a leek, gleaming like it had been carved out of spring itself.

A news helicopter hovered just beyond the shield’s edge, buffeted by turbulent air.

“—This is unbelievable, absolutely unbelievable—” Cassidy’s voice trembled with equal parts fear and practiced excitement as she clutched her microphone. The cameraman beside her fought to keep the creature in frame. “What you’re seeing behind me is a Category-Omega event. The city’s containment field is active, but casualties are still unknown. We’ve been told—”

Cassidy stopped mid-sentence, staring.

She gasped. “T-There she is. Ladies and gentlemen at home—our savior is here. Pretty Druid Guardian Mori Midori has arrived on the scene.”

Midori stood framed against the night sky, her dress woven from leaves and branches that rustled despite the still air.

She did not strike.

She raised her voice instead.

“Grooooooooar!” Midori roared.

Cassidy fumbled the earpiece at her neck. “W-we have an interpreter with us, live—who will gladly translate for our viewers at home.”

The interpreter’s voice cut in, crisp and a little too calm for the apocalypse outside the window.

“Midori is saying: ‘Please. I know you’re scared. I’m here now. This destruction must stop at once. People are being hurt.’”

The creature answered with a thunderous roar.

To the people watching at home, it was nothing but rage—raw sound that rattled bone and camera lens alike.

“GRRROAAAAAR!”

The interpreter hesitated, then spoke.

“Translation: ‘It is you, guardian of this land. I was warned by my council that you would come. I am deeply sorry, but I must take this land for my people. We find ourselves under attack by a force beyond our comprehension. As a king, I must place the safety of my people before my pride—and even my own principles. I hope you can understand.’”

Midori’s expression softened.

“Groar,” she replied gently.

The interpreter returned immediately.

“Midori is saying: ‘I do understand your pain. I really do. But you need to understand this: the people who live here fought for this place. They built it with their own hands. You cannot have it.’”

Midori lifted her shield slightly, as if offering it rather than threatening with it.

“‘But I will not leave you at death’s mercy. If you are willing to take me with you, I will do my best to help your world.’”

Another roar split the air.

“GROOOOOOOOOOOOAR!”

“Translation,” the interpreter said, voice lower now. “ ‘No. It is far too late. That creature’s power… I have faced powerful creatures, devastating magic in the past, yet it is unlike anything we have ever faced. Even now, I fear it may have followed me to your world. I am deeply sorry from the bottom of my heart, but I must put you down—now—and prepare my people for the coming war against the one with the hat.’”

Midori closed her eyes for a moment.

There was no annoyance on her face.

Only regret.

When her eyes opened again, there was no hesitation left in them.

The great lizard king put his mighty hands together and dropped them in a devastating hammer strike.

Midori planted her feet and raised her shield.

The impact rang like a bell.

With a sharp parry, she redirected the force back into the debris field, sending shattered concrete skidding like pebbles.

“GROOOOOOOOOOOOOAR!”

The interpreter spoke over the violent footage. “ ‘Please, remain still as I strike you. You will only make this harder than it needs to be.’”

The king kept coming.

Blow after blow.

Midori parried again and again, deflecting the brute strength into the ruins—until one deflection slammed toward the helicopter.

The cabin jolted.

“Wo-wow—pull us back! Get us to safety!” Cassidy yelled.

Midori’s gaze flicked upward. For the first time, her calm cracked.

“Groar,” she roared—low and solemn.

The interpreter didn’t need to hesitate this time.

“ ‘This can’t go on any further. I’m sorry.’”

Midori raised her sword to her face.

Her shield dissolved into a burst of verdant light and fused with the blade.

“Oh, Bastet,” she intoned, steady despite the chaos. “Grant me the key of any lock; turn any wall into a door; guide me toward evil’s heart.”

The weapon reshaped into a long, elegant spear—living green radiance crawling along its length.

The lizard king stared, awed.

“Groar!”

“Translation,” the interpreter murmured. “ ‘I see. You wish to battle our wills against each other for the fate of our worlds. It does seem fair. I accept your challenge.’”

Midori leveled the spear.

“This is my will. My oath. My love for all that lives and breathes.!”

Her voice sharpened.

“ExcaLEEKbur!”

She lunged.

The king roared in response and braced himself, inhaling the air around him like he meant to swallow the sky.

Then—perfect English, booming through the night.

“Atomic Quazar Blast!”

A torrent of energy erupted from his maw, a blinding beam that tore through the air and swallowed Midori whole.

She did not stop.

She flew straight through it.

The light intensified until even the helicopter cameras white-outed.

“Cassidy—Cassidy, we’ve lost visual!” the cameraman shouted.

As the glare faded, Midori came into view again—kneeling atop a ruined building, breathing hard, her dress scorched and withered.

Across from her, the great lizard stood motionless.

Then he placed one knee on the ground.

And bowed his head.

“Gr… groar.”

The interpreter spoke softly, almost reverently.

“ ‘Oh, how foolish I was. To call myself king, yet behave as a stubborn child. With this power of yours, oh great guardian of humankind, we may have stood a chance against the forces of evil. Alas, we shall never know. All due to me.’”

Both of the king’s knees faltered.

They hit the ground with a violent tremor.

A glowing, leaf-shaped hole burned clean through his chest.

He tried to steady himself against a nearby building.

It crumbled under his weight.

“Groar…”

The interpreter’s voice tightened. “ ‘I beg of you, honorable guardian. When news of my death reaches my son, he will surely seek vengeance. Convince him otherwise. Save our people from the mistakes of their foolish king.’”

Midori rose.

Her spear separated back into sword and shield. Despite the weapon being pristine, she performed a solemn chiburi, flicking unseen blood from the blade.

“Groar,” she answered, bowing her head.

Then, in clear English—quiet, absolute:

“On my honor, it shall be done.”

The Kaiju released one final sound.

And this time, he did not need translating.

“…Thank you,” he whispered.

Instead of collapsing, his body dissolved—breaking apart into a cloud of glittering magic that drifted upward and vanished into the sky.

Midori took one breath. Whispered a quiet prayer for the fallen king.

A black cat appeared at her feet, weaving between her legs.

Without explanation, it produced a thin cigarette and took a long drag.

“Done with your theatrics?” the cat said, exhaling smoke that curled into lazy circles. “The show must go on, sweetcheeks.”

Midori’s seriousness melted into a warm smile. “Every life is worth fighting for, Love. Every life is worth mourning for.”

“By all means,” Love replied. “Save the sermon. We still have work to do.”

Midori took flight, soaring toward the helicopter.

Cassidy leaned forward, breathless. “Miss Midori! Midori-san! Please—for LCN—we thank you once again for saving us! Do you have any words for the children watching at home?”

Midori hesitated slightly, then nodded.

“Uh, sure. Well—always extend a helping hand. Always respect your elders. And—oh! Of course.”

She struck a bright pose: legs set wide, a sideways peace sign, her eye framed between her fingers.

“Don’t forget to eat your veggies!”

With that, she vanished into the clouds.

Cassidy kept talking—her voice a blur of awe and speculation—but the broadcast faded out, leaving only the image of a city still standing.

“Don’t forget to eat your veggies!”

The little girl—barely six years old—copied the pose perfectly. She stood in the middle of the tatami room with her fingers framing her eye, her balance wavering but not her smile. The glow of the television flickered, painting her face in soft greens and golds.

She spun on her heel and ran down the hallway.

“Mommy! Mommy!” she shouted. “Look—look! Miss Midori won again!”

Her mother glanced up from the kitchen doorway, drying her hands on a worn towel. “Oh? Really, honey?” she said, her voice warm but distant. “Amazing. Just like last time.” She let the words drag, gentle and automatic.

The girl bounced on her heels, barely able to contain herself. “Did you know? I’m going to be just like Miss Midori when I grow up!”

The woman paused.

The concerns of adulthood pressed through her expression. The quiet hardships of life pushed against her optimism—but her smile never faltered.

Then she exhaled.

She let herself believe.

The mother crouched down and lifted the child into her arms, holding her close.

“You will,” she said softly, pressing her cheek to the girl’s hair. “You surely will, my little song.”

“Bastet! Guide me into the hearts… of all of you!”

The carefully arranged pink and gold lights warmed the establishment. As the music began, Tamami’s smile widened on cue.

Her costume echoed Midori from her childhood, but only in outline. Leaves replaced by glossy plastic. Branches reduced to clean seams and molded edges. Durable. Washable. Cheap to replace.

Just like she felt.

Men in pressed shirts and loosened ties sat around small round tables, clapping politely with the rhythm. Some of the more extroverted ones shook Midori merchandise above their heads. Their faces were tired. Their smiles earnest.

Tamami sang and danced.

She hit every mark her exhausted body could manage after a full shift of performances. At this point, her body remembered the choreography better than she did.

Her mind drifted somewhere safer.

*Half an hour. Just… half an hour more.*

The song came to a close. The applause grew louder.

Tamami smiled and waved, forcing it.

Then a voice cut through the room.

“Woooo! Encore! Encore!”

Tamami froze.

Her smile collapsed. Gravity snapped back into place.

“C’mon, you bozos! Encore! Encore!”

As if commanded, the salarymen joined in, chanting along with her.

Tamami searched for the voice.

A phone, raised too casually.

A sharp, delighted laugh.

Her stomach dropped.

On the last seat, half-hidden behind the sparse crowd, a young woman with dyed-blonde hair giggled openly. Heavy makeup. A deliberately provocative schoolgirl uniform that hadn’t seen a high school in years.

Kaminari Torey.

A delinquent. A vandal.

And worse—

One of her students at Lincourt Community College.

Torey waved at her with a shit-eating grin.

Tamami turned and ran backstage.

The crowd booed, disappointed.

Backstage, Tamami pressed her forehead against the wall, clutching her chest. Her breath came too fast. Thoughts collided—being exposed, being fired, being sent back to her family’s farm in rural Japan.

She shook them away.

*Be an adult.*

She turned around to confront Torey—

—who was already there.

“What’s looking good, cooking?” Torey said, smiling.

“Huh?” Tamami blurted.

“What’s up, Miss Tatami-san?”

“You either say *Miss* or *-san*—ah, forget it.” Tamami swallowed. “Torey, please. Delete that. I don’t know what you’re planning, but you have to delete it.”

“Oh, this?” Torey tilted her head, mock-thoughtful. “This’ll keep me company on those long nights at sea.”

She tapped her phone. “Hmm.”

“How about this? You go back out there and sing one more song. For me. No recordings, Pinky. Promise.”

“W-What? I’m your teacher, you can’t—”

“Ah-ah-ah!” Torey cut in. “Right now you’re Midori-san, remember? And *moi* is a customer.” She clasped her hands under her chin in a faux-innocent pose. “Don’t you want to make the customer happy?”

Tamami froze.

Then—

“One song.”

Her voice was steady.

Torey blinked, then nodded like a parrot.

---

This time, the lights felt brighter.

Most of the café had already gone dark. Only the stage lights remained, reflected entirely on her.

Tamami sang.

She sang like she almost never did anymore. Like back when dressing up as Midori had felt exciting. Fun.

She danced until her body burned.

The small crowd went wild.

So did Torey.

Tamami moved with intention. With determination. Her voice was sharp and strong.

For the first time that night, she didn’t have to force her smile.

The song ended.

The applause was louder than before.

Tamami bowed once and walked straight to the dressing room. The crowd murmured among themselves as they filtered out.

Back in the dressing room, Tamami changed into her brown suit.

Torey wandered in like she belonged.

“That was *tots* fabulous,” she said. “The dancing? God.”

“Delete it,” Tamami said flatly.

“What? But you were having fun! We could totally repea—”

*Thud!*

Tamami punched the metal door of her locker.

A small dent bloomed in the steel.

“Oh—right, right.” Torey laughed, a little too fast. “Did I say something? So silly. I meant—I already deleted it.”

Tamami picked up her things and headed for the exit.

Torey followed.

“Like, L-M-A-O, teach,” Torey said as they passed the closed curtain of the café. “What are you even doing here?”

“You should address me as sens—” Tamami stopped.

She turned.

“What are *you* doing here, Torey?”

The laughter died.

“Oh—well, you know,” Torey said quickly. “My loser radar went crazy when I passed by and I thought it’d be funny to—”

Tamami’s manager stepped out, Midori’s folded costume tucked under her arm. She pulled the metal curtain shut and locked it with a heavy clang.

“Goodnight, Hanami-sensei! Bye, Torey! See you next Friday!”

Silence.

Torey stared at the closed curtain. Her face didn’t redden. It didn’t move.

It was pure defeat.

“Should I walk you home?” Tamami asked.

“Yes,” Torey said quietly. “Please.”


---

The city had quieted down. Neon signs buzzed. Traffic lights clicked from red to green for no one in particular.

Tamami and Torey walked side by side. For a while, neither of them spoke.

“…So,” Torey said at last, hands in her pockets. “You, uh. You really work there? Like, for realsies?”

“For realsies,” Tamami replied flatly.

“LOL, why?” Torey asked, not actually laughing.

“It pays the rest,” Tamami said. “You don’t make a living as a history teacher.”

“Bet,” Torey replied dryly.

“The other teachers can’t know,” Tamami continued. “The faculty would see it as tainting the college’s prestige. I’d lose my job.”

“Bet,” Torey repeated.

They walked another block in silence. Only their footsteps filled the street.

“You—uh,” Torey said. “You were tots cool up there, y’know? Like, legit. That was pretty lit.”

“…Thank you?” Tamami replied, the generational gap obvious.

“I mean, I like all that stuff. Singing, dancing, the whole deal. I’d suck at it, but it looks really fun.”

Tamami glanced at her, genuinely curious.

“And the attention from all those losers must feel, like, super validating.”

“Absolutely,” Tamami said, smirking. “Never felt more realized.”

“Ya know,” Torey went on, “when I was little I totes wanted to be an idol. Like, I’d watch—”

“Midori on TV,” they both said at the same time.

Torey stiffened.

“…Yeah. She’s like my hecking hero. Totally my role model.”

“Yes… clearly,” Tamami replied, eyeing her outfit.

They kept walking. Somehow, the night felt lighter.

“I was a fat kid,” Torey blurted out suddenly. “Like, really fat. Like, ‘give me a hand and I’ll eat your elbow’ fat.” She didn’t slow down. “That’s why I figured I’d never fit into the idol world. Like, for realzies and also meta—meta—whatever. You know what I mean. And my father’s this super respected business guy here, so he’d find it dishonorable or some BS. And mommy?” She scoffed. “She says being an idol is a job. And only poor people have jobs.”

Tamami winced.

“What about you, Tatami-san?” Torey asked. “Why are you a girl-failure? Well—more like hag-failure.”

“Not my name,” Tamami said. “But why do you think I consider myself a failure?”

Torey just stared at her.

“I moved to Lincourt City when I was a teenager,” Tamami said, conceding. “I’d always been obsessed with the place because of Mori Midori. I thought maybe, if I made a name for myself as an idol, I could meet her someday.”

“And?” Torey asked.

“Being an independent idol doesn’t pay the bills,” Tamami said. “And as a foreign, lone teenager, I was the perfect target for sleazy managers. I wasn’t taking chances. So I started teaching to make ends meet.” She exhaled. “Before I knew it, most of my time was spent in classrooms. My dreams faded.”

“Wow,” Torey muttered. “What a bummer.”

“Bet,” Tamami replied, sarcastically.

They turned a corner.

“Hey, beauty. Hey! You with the nice ass!”

Tamami stopped and turned, her glare pure disdain.

“That is no way to speak to a lady,” she said coldly.

Two drunkards leaned against a wall. Their grins faltered.

“E-Excuse us, ma’am,” one stammered. “We meant to talk to your daughter.”

“Hah!” Torey laughed.

“Excuse me?” Tamami snapped. “I’m seven years older than her!”

“How do you know my age? LOL,” Torey said.

“I—I don’t get it, lady,” the other drunk slurred. “You want us to hit on you too? I mean, I like ’em old.”

Tamami didn’t answer. She grabbed Torey’s arm and pulled her forward.

“Keep moving.”

Footsteps followed.

“Aw, don’t be like that.”

“Where you goin’?”

“We can hit on you too, old lady!” one called.

Torey stopped, yanking her wrist free.

“She told you to get the fuck away from us, you fucking bozo,” Torey snarled.

She pulled out a gun. Sideways. Shaking.

"Technically speaking she never di-" One of the men said.

“Well, I’m saying it now!” Torey screamed. “SO-BACK-THE-FUCK-UP! I will kill you! I’ve killed before and liked it!”

Everything froze.

Tamami didn’t know what to do. She didn’t want one of her students hurt. She didn’t want anyone hurt.

The men stared. One of them wet himself.

“Ugh,” Torey said. “Gross. Get outta here before I make you spill other stuff.”

They ran. Leaving behind booze, fear, and shame.

Silence snapped back into place.

Tamami stared at Torey.

“…Why in the world do you have a gun?”

Torey smirked as she pulled the trigger.

Click.

A flame flickered.

“Heh,” Torey chuckled.

“…You shouldn’t smoke either,” Tamami said.

Far from the café, the drunkards wandered down a quieter street, irritation still clinging to them like the smell of alcohol.

“Crazy bitches,” one muttered, kicking at a loose can. “No sense of humor.”

“Yeah,” the other replied with a laugh that came out too loud for the empty street. “All that effort and no fun at all.”

They turned a corner and slowed.

Someone stood beneath a streetlight further ahead, unmoving, framed by the weak yellow glow. At first glance, the figure looked inviting enough — a generous silhouette, long legs, curves catching the light in just the right way.

One of them whistled.

“Hey, bombshell,” he called out, confidence returning with the familiar script. “Wanna have some fun?”

The figure did not react.

They exchanged grins and kept walking, words tumbling out easier now that they had decided what they were looking at.

“C’mon,” the other added. “Don’t be shy.”

As they drew closer, the streetlight steadied, and the illusion unraveled.

The figure was not a woman.

It was a tall, unnaturally thin man, dressed in an extravagant suit that looked tailored for a different era and a different audience altogether. A cane rested lightly in one gloved hand, more accessory than support, and a tophat sat perfectly atop his head, untouched by the night air. Around him lingered a faint purple hue, subtle enough to dismiss at first glance, yet persistent enough to make their eyes ache if they stared too long.

The men slowed, uncertainty creeping back in.

“…What the hell?” one of them muttered.

The man smiled.

Not politely. Not awkwardly.

His mouth stretched far wider than it should have, exposing rows of yellowed teeth in a grin that suggested delight rather than friendliness, as though he had just been offered a gift he’d been hoping for all evening.

“Fun?” he said, voice bright and almost eager. “Oh, how marvelous. I do so adore fun.”

The men’s laughter faltered.

“Tell me,” the man continued, tilting his head just enough to feel deliberate, “may I interest you in something… far more fun than what you had in mind?”

Neither of them answered.

The streetlight flickered once.

When it steadied again, the corner was empty, save for a lingering unease that refused to name itself and the sense that something had gone terribly, irrevocably wrong.

They girls kept walking, side by side, the city thinning around them as storefronts gave way to closed offices and darkened windows, the air cooler now that the crowds were gone.

“So yeah,” Torey went on, clearly warming to the sound of her own voice, swinging her arms as she walked. “I mean, people think it’s all fancy and glamorous and whatever, but it’s actually kind of a pain when everyone already assumes your entire life is mapped out for you. Like, there’s expectations, you know? Meetings, dinners, being introduced to people I don’t care about, everyone acting like I’m supposed to be impressed.”

Tamami hummed noncommittally.

“And it’s not like I asked to be born into it,” Torey continued, oblivious. “It’s just how things are. I used to hang out with him all the time when I was little, but now he’s always busy, and whenever we do talk it’s all about ‘the future’ and ‘responsibility’ and blah blah blah. It’s exhausting.”

“That does sound taxing,” Tamami said, her tone flat in a way that made it impossible to tell whether she was sympathizing or mocking her.

Torey nodded earnestly. “Right? Thank you.”

They walked another block.

“So,” Tamami said at last, eyes forward. “If your life is already… arranged, why are you studying at a community college?”

Torey opened her mouth to answer.

A sound cut through the night.

Whistling.

Off-key, repetitive, drifting toward them from behind.

Torey groaned. “Ugh. Please don’t tell me it’s those weezos again.”

She turned around, already pulling the lighter from her pocket, annoyance replacing whatever vulnerability had been building. “Hey! Dickheads! What did I tell yah earlier!?”

The whistling didn’t stop.

The figures stepped closer, into the pale wash of moonlight.

Tamami’s stomach tightened.

They weren’t men anymore.

Their heads had warped into obscene, gland-like shapes, swollen and smooth, their necks stretched long and loose, hanging slack as they walked and swaying in wide arcs that made their bodies move wrong. From their throats dangled two heavy, rounded masses that bounced with each step, tugging their posture forward. Their hands and feet ended in thick, curved claws that scraped softly against the pavement as they advanced.

They kept whistling.

Then they noticed the girls.

The change was immediate.

Their necks stiffened and drew upright in a sudden, sickening motion, the flesh pulling taut as their whistling sharpened into something frantic and eager. Without warning, they broke into a run, claws striking asphalt, bodies lurching toward them with horrifying enthusiasm.

Torey froze.

The lighter slipped from her fingers and hit the ground with a useless clatter.

“W—what the—” she stammered, voice cracking. “What the fuck is that?”

Tamami didn’t answer.

She grabbed Torey by the arm and pulled her hard.

“Move,” she said, dry and sharp. “Now.”

They ran.

Footsteps thundered behind them, wet and uneven, the whistling echoing off the buildings in a way that made it impossible to tell how close the creatures really were. They turned a corner too fast, nearly slamming into a wall of brick, and for a split second Tamami thought they were done for.

“Shit!” Torey swore.

But Tamami yanked her sideways before they could stop, dragging her into what looked like an alley at first glance, narrow and foul-smelling, trash bags stacked high along the walls, the air thick with rot and damp concrete.

They ran.

And then they realized it wasn’t an alley at all.

The passage opened abruptly into an empty gap between buildings, a forgotten pocket of concrete too wide to be a corridor and too enclosed to be a street, unfinished and useless, a place where nothing was meant to happen and no one was meant to stand.

There was nowhere left to go.

Behind them, the whistling grew louder.

Closer.

“What do we do, what do we do—my phone—my dad—why isn’t there any signal!?” Torey babbled, staring at her screen and shaking it like it might suddenly remember how to work. “We’re in the middle of Lincourt! This doesn’t make sense—this never happens—”

The distorted figures advanced into the space, their elongated necks swaying obscenely as they closed in, claws scraping against the concrete with lazy confidence, as if they already knew the outcome.

Hanami swallowed.

“I will… distract them,” she said, her voice steady in a way that surprised even herself. “Then you run.”

Torey spun around. “What!? Are you insane!?”

“It’s my duty,” Hanami replied, adjusting her grip on her suitcase as her hands began to tremble. “As a teacher.”

The creatures lunged forward, closing the distance far too quickly.

“No—wait!” Torey screamed, turning back toward them. “HEY! You fucks! Here! Over here!”

The movement was instant.

Too fast for Torey to react.

But not for Hanami.

She stepped in front of her without thinking, throwing the suitcase up in a useless attempt at protection. The claws tore through leather and fabric like paper, shredding the case, her suit, and then her flesh in the same brutal motion. The impact knocked the air from her lungs as pain exploded through her body, hot and overwhelming, blood spilling freely onto the concrete.

“NO!” Torey screamed as Hanami collapsed. She dropped beside her, throwing her arms over Hanami’s body, shielding her with her own. “HELP! SOMEBODY! ANYONE! STAY AWAY YOU FUCKS—LEAVE HER ALONE!”

Hanami barely heard her.

Her vision blurred as the world narrowed, thoughts drifting uncontrollably through memories she hadn’t meant to revisit — the fields back home, the television glow of a city she’d fallen in love with from afar, the things she never became, the songs she never sang properly, the life she’d kept telling herself she was fine with.

The creatures reared back, ready to strike again.

Torey squeezed her eyes shut, clinging to Hanami with everything she had.

Then the night exploded in green light.

The alley was flooded with it, sharp and brilliant, and when the light faded the creatures lay severed on the ground, their bodies cut cleanly in half. As they twitched and stilled, their grotesque forms collapsed back into ordinary human shapes, as if the nightmare had been peeled away at the last second.

Torey looked up, tears streaming down her face.

Hanami did too, her vision swimming.

Standing at the mouth of the alley was Mori Midori.

For a second, Torey’s instincts took over. She snapped a dozen photos in rapid succession, even a shaky selfie, before reality crashed back into her and she shoved the phone away, panic returning full force.

“Please!” she sobbed. “Midori-sensei! My friend—she’s—she’s like dying, I don’t know what to do!”

Midori’s stern expression softened as she knelt beside Hanami, though up close it was clear she was far from unscathed. Her dress of leaves was torn and withering, her movements slower, her breathing heavy, as if she had come straight from another battle.

She examined Hanami’s wounds, her brow furrowing. “This… this isn’t just dark magic.”

A black cat in a neat bowtie stepped into view behind her, puffing calmly on a cigar. “Who cares?” he said. “Those two were worryingly close to our trail. We need to keep moving.”

“We can’t just leave two citizens to their demise, Love,” Midori replied quietly.

“Sure we can,” the cat said flatly. “It would, in fact, be very simple.”

“Leave us?” Torey choked out. “Why!? What’s going on!? Please don’t leave us!” She clutched Hanami tighter, tears streaking her face. “I can’t die next to someone poor—no offense.” She glanced down at Hanami, her expression sobering for a brief second.

“Mi… dori,” Hanami murmured, smiling faintly despite the pain. Somehow, she wasn’t afraid.

Midori’s expression darkened. “There is something I can do,” she said slowly, “but it may not work.”

“Oh no you won’t,” Love snapped. “You’re almost out of primater. Let’s just go.”

Midori placed her hands gently over Hanami’s wounds. “Love, every life is wor—”

“Shut it,” the cat interrupted, exhaling smoke as he stepped forward. “I’m tired of hearing it.”

He placed his paws over Midori’s hands, black energy intertwining with her green glow. “This hag’s life better be worth the effort.”

“I’m… barely… in my thirties,” Hanami whispered weakly.

Energy flowed from them, green and black spiraling together before seeping into Hanami’s body, shifting to a pale blue as it settled deep within her, finally vanishing near her lower abdomen.

Torey gasped as the wounds began to close. Midori smiled faintly, relief flashing across her face just before her legs gave out. Torey rushed to catch her, guiding her against the wall.

“Midori-sensei! Are you alright!?”

“Yes,” Midori said, breathless. “I’m sorry for worrying you. The wound was fatal. Healing it took… a great toll. I just need to rest.”

“From us, you mean,” Love muttered, taking another drag.

Hanami’s eyes snapped open.

She shot upright with a gasp.

“Tatami-sensei! You’re alive!”

“Tamami,” she corrected automatically, then frowned. “And it’s sens—oh.” She grabbed Torey by the shoulders. “You doofus, what were you doing risking your life like that for me!? I told you to run!”

“I knew I sensed a noble heart,” Midori said weakly.

Hanami’s breath caught as she looked at her, really looked at her, and her knees gave out as she knelt beside the guardian, clutching her hand.

“No, Midori. This… this is my fault.”

“In that, we agree,” Love said dryly. “My best fighter is out of commission saving you. What are you going to do about it?”

“Love!” Midori protested.

“No,” Hanami said firmly, bowing deeply despite her shaking body. “Mister Love is right. I am in your debt. I shall repay you as soon as possible.”

A thunderous roar echoed through the city, shaking the alley walls.

Love’s ears flattened as he glanced toward the sound. “Hear that, hag?” he said, forcing a smirk. “That might be your hospital bill.”
